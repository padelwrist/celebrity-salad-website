BIG ROOM, SHORT RULES

Games for large groups that do not leave half the room waiting.

The bigger the group, the more important teams, short turns and shared attention become.

The rule for large-group games

A game that works brilliantly with six people can collapse with sixteen if everyone has to wait for a long individual turn. Good large-group games compress downtime by using teams, simultaneous participation or very short rounds.

Optimise for participation, not complexityIn a crowded room, a simple game that keeps 18 people watching and shouting is better than a clever game that fully engages four people at a time.

Choose by group size

10–12

Two-team guessing games work almost exactly as they do with smaller groups.

13–20

Shorten turns and reduce individual submissions so rounds do not sprawl.

20+

Use clear teams, a host and formats where the whole room can follow the action.

Eight games that scale well

1. Celebrity Salad

Two teams take timed turns guessing a shared pool of celebrity names through Describe It, One Word and Act It. Everyone can follow the same answers, so people stay invested even when it is not their turn.

For 12+ people, use two or three names per player and shorter turns. See the official rules.

2. Reverse Charades

The whole team acts while one person guesses. More people are physically involved at once than in ordinary Charades, which makes it strong for big groups.

3. Team Pictionary

Run two drawing stations in parallel or alternate rapid turns. Keep prompts simple enough for the wider room to follow.

4. Rapid-fire categories

Teams alternate answers inside a category with a strict time limit. Almost no setup and no one person dominates for long.

5. Human bingo

Give people a grid of facts or experiences and let them mingle to find matching people. Best when the goal is helping a large group mix rather than creating one central spectacle.

6. Quiz in teams

Classic for a reason. Keep rounds short, vary the topics and avoid questions so specialist that only one table can participate.

7. Telephone Pictionary

Players alternate writing and drawing along a chain, then reveal how the original idea mutated. Works especially well when multiple chains run at the same time.

8. This-or-that corners

Give two choices and ask people to move to a side of the room. It needs space but almost no explanation and can quickly warm up a crowd.

How long should turns be?

Large groups benefit from shorter turns. In Celebrity Salad, 30 or 45 seconds can keep momentum high when there are many clue-givers. Longer 60 or 90 second turns suit smaller teams where each person needs more time in the spotlight.

How many teams?

Two teams are easiest for a central party game because everyone knows who is playing and who is guessing. More teams can reduce team size but usually create more scorekeeping and more waiting. Split into extra teams only when the format genuinely supports simultaneous play.

Do you need a host?

Once the room gets above roughly 15 people, one person keeping the rules and transitions clear is useful even if they are also playing. The host does not need a script; they just need to stop the group renegotiating the rules every five minutes.
